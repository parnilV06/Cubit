In the spring of 1974, an architecture professor in Budapest, Hungary, carved twenty-six small wooden blocks and joined them with rubber bands. He was trying to build a physical object whose pieces could move independently without the structure falling apart.

He did not intend to build the best-selling toy in human history. He was simply trying to help his students visualize three-dimensional geometry.

That prototype became the **Rubik's Cube**.

> [!NOTE]
> When Ernő Rubik first scrambled his prototype, it took him over a month of dedicated study to figure out how to solve it back to its starting state.

---

## 1. The Invention in Budapest (1974–1977)

Ernő Rubik taught interior design and architecture at the Academy of Applied Arts in Budapest. Intrigued by structural mechanics, he experimented with different internal core designs until he created a mechanism where interlocking plastic pieces gripped a central six-axis spindle.

In 1975, Rubik patented his invention in Hungary under the name **Bűvös Kocka** (*Magic Cube*). The first commercial batches appeared in Budapest toy shops in late 1977, quickly capturing the attention of Hungarian mathematicians and puzzle lovers.

## 2. The 1980s Global Craze

In 1979, the puzzle caught the eye of international toy specialists at the Nuremberg Toy Fair. Ideal Toy Corporation licensed the design, changed its name to the **Rubik's Cube**, and launched it internationally in early 1980.

What followed was an unprecedented global phenomenon:

- Between 1980 and 1982, more than **100 million cubes** were sold worldwide.
- Solution books topped international bestseller lists for months.
- In June 1982, the first **World Rubik's Cube Championship** was held in Budapest. Nineteen competitors from around the globe competed on original stock cubes. **Minh Thai** of the United States won with a winning time of **22.95 seconds**.

---

## 3. The Internet Revival and the Birth of the WCA

Following the initial 1980s craze, public interest waned, but a dedicated community of mathematicians and puzzle enthusiasts kept developing faster methods.

In the late 1990s, the internet transformed cubing:

1. **Online Method Sharing**: Jessica Fridrich published the modern CFOP speedsolving system online, giving cubers a standardized roadmap to sub-20 second solves.
2. **The 2003 World Championship**: After a 21-year hiatus, cubers gathered in Toronto for the second World Championship. Dan Knights won with an average of 20.00 seconds.
3. **The World Cube Association (2004)**: Ron van Bruchem and Tyson Mao founded the **WCA** to maintain official competition rules, certify timing equipment, and track global rankings across all puzzle disciplines.

---

## 4. Modern Speedcubing

Today, speedcubing is a global sport practiced by hundreds of thousands of competitors in over 140 countries.

Modern speedcubes bear little resemblance to the stiff plastic cubes of 1980:

- **Magnetic Positioning**: Tiny neodymium magnets in every piece snap turns into perfect alignment.
- **Adjustable Tensioning**: Dual-adjustment systems allow cubers to customize spring elasticity and distance down to tenths of a millimeter.
- **MagLev Technology**: Opposing magnetic rings replace traditional metal springs, eliminating friction and spring noise.

These innovations, combined with modern fingertricks and algorithm development, have driven the official 3x3 single world record down to **sub-3.2 seconds**.

## 6. Key Takeaways

- **Originated as a teaching tool**: Built by Ernő Rubik to understand spatial mechanics.
- **Cultural icon**: One of the best-selling puzzle games of all time.
- **Evolved into an esport**: Governed by the World Cube Association with standardized rules and worldwide rankings.
