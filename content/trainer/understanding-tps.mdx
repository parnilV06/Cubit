In speedcubing discussions, you will frequently hear competitors ask: *"What was your TPS on that solve?"*

**TPS** stands for **Turns Per Second**. It is the standard velocity metric used across speedcubing to measure how fast a solver executes moves on the puzzle.

Understanding what TPS is, how it is calculated, and its relationship to move efficiency is essential for analyzing your solves and breaking through time plateaus.

> [!NOTE]
> TPS measures pure mechanical execution rate. While elite speedcubers can burst above 10 to 12 TPS during last-layer algorithms, their overall solve time is defined by how few moves they use and how continuously they turn throughout the entire solve.

---

## 1. How TPS is Calculated

TPS is calculated by dividing your total move count by your total solve time in seconds:

$$\text{TPS} = \frac{\text{Total Move Count}}{\text{Solve Time in Seconds}}$$

### Move Metric Standards in Speedcubing

When counting moves, cubing software and analysis tools use three primary metrics:

1. **HTM (Half-Turn Metric)**: Any face turn counts as 1 move (e.g. `R`, `R'`, and `R2` all count as 1 move). Slice turns (`M`) count as 2 moves (`R L'`).
2. **STM (Slice-Turn Metric)**: Single face turns, double turns, and single slice turns (`M`, `E`, `S`) each count as 1 move.
3. **ETM (Execution-Turn Metric)**: Counts every single physical action, including double turns (`U2` = 2 finger flicks) and whole-cube rotations (`y` = 1 movement). This is the most accurate reflection of physical hand workload.

### Practical Calculation Example

If you complete a solve with **56 moves (STM)** in **16.0 seconds**:

$$\text{TPS} = \frac{56 \text{ moves}}{16.0 \text{ s}} = 3.5 \text{ TPS}$$

## 2. The TPS Trap: Why Higher TPS Does Not Mean Faster Solves

The biggest misconception among beginners is believing that turning faster is the only way to get faster times.

Consider this direct comparison between two solvers:

| Solver | Solving Method | Move Count | Turning Speed (TPS) | Total Solve Time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Solver A (High TPS, Low Efficiency)** | Beginner Layer-by-Layer | 110 moves | **5.5 TPS** | **20.0 seconds** |
| **Solver B (Moderate TPS, High Efficiency)** | CFOP Method | 50 moves | **3.5 TPS** | **14.3 seconds** |

Solver B turns **36% slower** than Solver A, yet finishes **5.7 seconds faster** because their method requires less than half the moves!

Move efficiency is a force multiplier on TPS. Increasing your turning speed without improving your efficiency produces diminishing returns, hand fatigue, and frequent lockups.

## 3. Real-World TPS Benchmarks by Solve Phase

TPS is never constant throughout a solve. It naturally fluctuates depending on the cognitive load of each phase:

```
[ Phase 1: Cross ]
• Typical TPS: 2.0 to 4.5 TPS
• Focus: Executing planned inspection moves accurately.

[ Phase 2: First Two Layers (F2L) ]
• Typical TPS: 3.5 to 6.5 TPS
• Focus: Controlled turning to maintain unbroken lookahead.

[ Phase 3: Last Layer (OLL & PLL) ]
• Typical TPS: 6.0 to 12.0+ TPS
• Focus: Explosive muscle-memory algorithm execution.
```

## 5. How to Track and Analyze Your TPS

1. **Use the Cubit Timer**: Record timed sessions to track your overall solve durations and consistency.
2. **Practice Reconstruction**: Periodically count the moves in a recorded solve to calculate your true TPS.
3. **Diagnose Bottlenecks**: If your overall TPS is low despite fast algorithm execution, your solve is suffering from search pauses or inefficient cross solutions.

---

## 6. Key Takeaways

- **TPS measures turns per second**: Calculated as $\text{Move Count} \div \text{Time}$.
- **Efficiency multiplied by TPS equals speed**: Halving your move count cuts your time in half without needing faster hands.
- **TPS varies by phase**: Cross and F2L require controlled turning for lookahead; Last Layer allows maximum algorithm bursts.
- **You are ready for CFOP**: Now that you understand speedcubing fundamentals, the next step is learning the **CFOP method**: the world-standard speedcubing system that cuts move count down to ~55 moves.
